New theory
There may be a second case here — a Marvin claim
Raised unprompted by one attorney and recognised immediately by another, which is the strongest capability signal of the day. California abolished common-law marriage, but Marvin v. Marvin (1976) lets unmarried partners enforce agreements about support and property. Written promises plus payments actually made is the raw material. This is not capped at the ledger figure — it is a different vehicle with a different ceiling, and it reframes the whole matter from defence-only to defence-plus-claim.
Marvin is a separate civil action — and the clock may not have started
Timing
It is a contract claim between non-spouses, so it is filed in civil court, not family court, and there is no dissolution here to attach it to. Limitations: 4 years on a written contract (CCP §337), 2 on oral or implied (§339). Accrual normally runs from the end of the relationship — but where the other side kept performing after separation, Cochran v. Cochran (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 1115 holds the clock does not start until performance stops. Payments were made. Whorton v. Dillingham (1988) is the same-sex Marvin authority. None of this is urgent this week — which is exactly why it must not distract from the 19th.
Reframe
The continuance is a strategy, not a defeat — move it to the positive column
You filed this under negatives. Both attorneys leaned toward it, one at roughly 70%. The cost is real: under §245(c) the order stays in force through a continuance, so it means longer under it. But the alternative is walking into a contested evidentiary hearing underprepared and risking a five-year order with findings that would poison the Marvin claim and arm counterclaims. The trade is weeks against years. Two specialists landing there independently is your answer. What to insist on is a short, purposeful continuance with a work plan attached — not open-ended drift.
Unresolved
One firm has a prior connection to the other side — get it in writing
An attorney at one firm previously worked at a firm where the petitioner's spouse was a partner, and volunteered that the other side may be interested in settling. That may be ordinary inference from experience. It may not be. Three questions before any money moves: did you work with him directly and on what; will the firm run a formal conflict check and give me the result in writing; and how do you know what the other side wants — is that inference or information?
Still open
The criminal lane has not been touched
Nobody has been called on the criminal side. Family firms correctly say they don't do it — that was always the two-counsel design. But anything filed in family court is discoverable by a prosecutor, so the shield needs to exist before anything is filed under oath. Two verified certified criminal specialists sit at one number and it takes five minutes. Do it first thing.

If you lose your place entirely

“I'm the respondent in a contested restraining order case, my hearing is the nineteenth, and I need a consultation this week.”

That sentence alone does the whole job. Then stop, and let them ask.

If they ask what the case is about

“It's a contested restraining order and I'm the respondent. I'd rather give the detail to the attorney than over the phone — but I can tell you it's a documentary case and the evidence is already organised.”

If you get voicemail

If they ask your budget

“I have a substantial amount available at signing and can pay the balance on a schedule. I'd rather hear your number first than anchor you to mine.”

The fastest exit and the best exit are not the same door

Wanting out is the most understandable thing about this week and the most likely thing to make you choose badly. A five-year order with findings costs more than three extra weeks under a temporary one.

  • Never accept a verbal fee. Written, quoted by phase, or it didn't happen.
  • Never tell one firm another's number before you have both in writing.
  • Never consult a screener. Book the slot; save the facts for the attorney.
  • Zero contact with the petitioner or any protected person, in either direction, through anyone.
