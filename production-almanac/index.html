Chapter 01 · Shape · Architecture

Depth 3 · Build Ready 9 min deep-dive

01

Story Architecture.

A long episode holds when every turn changes the question the viewer is carrying.

Full Chapter →

Choose the controlling question first.

Build acts around changed understanding.

Reset before the viewer gets lost.

Cut brilliance that leaves the spine.

Key Rule

Every act must change the viewer's answer to the same question. If an act only adds information, it is probably a folder, not a story turn.

Steal This

Before research gets pretty, write the sentence below. Then make every chapter beat defend its right to stay.

“This is the story of [mechanism] told through [path], because [cost].”

Three Words That Carry The Chapter

Question.

The thing the episode is actually investigating — not the topic.

Line.

The path through the subject: a person, memo, shipment, border, court case, agency decision or money trail.

Turn.

The moment understanding changes; a good turn reorganizes the facts already on the table rather than adding one.

The Folder Test · Run It On Every Act

Does this act change the viewer's answer to the controlling question?

→→

Yes

A story turn. Keep it, and place it where it reorganizes what came before.

No

A folder. Save it, cut it, or make it a separate upload. Information without a changed read is a tangent.

Failure Modes

The chronology trap. Time order is not story order. Use chronology when cause depends on sequence, not because the folder starts in 1947.

The all-star sidebar. A brilliant tangent that does not change the line is still a tangent. Save it, cut it, or make it a separate upload.

The fake climax. A loud reveal without changed understanding is a lighting cue pretending to be structure.

The moral fog machine. If the viewer cannot state who did what, to whom, with what consequence, the episode is hiding inside mood.

“A chapter earns its length by changing the viewer's map.”

Value plus cause

A controlling idea is a value and the reason it holds. “Corruption is bad” is a value. “The auditors were paid by the audited” is a cause. If the spine sentence has no because, it is a topic.

Angela Phillips, Good Writing for Journalists, ch. 2 (citing McKee)

Four ways to move the middle

Acts sag when every beat is just more. Seger names four action points: the obstacle (the obvious explanation fails), the complication (a fact whose bill arrives later), the reversal (the read flips), the twist. Label each mid-episode beat; three obstacles in a row is a list.

Linda Seger, Making a Good Script Great, ch. 4

Plant it, then cash it

Cohesion comes from foreshadowing and payoff, recurring motifs, repetition, contrast. Show the memo at minute 2 that convicts at minute 22. A payoff with no plant reads as a lucky find; a plant with no payoff is clutter.

Seger, ch. 7

Intention and obstacle

Sorkin's whole test for a scene: somebody wants something, something is in the way. It works on ministries and central banks too. A segment where no actor wants anything is background.

Aaron Sorkin interview in Considering Aaron Sorkin (ed. Fahy)

Chronology is the causal structure

Tuchman defends dates as the thing that “establish sequence — what precedes and what follows — thereby leading toward an understanding of cause and effect.” Leave strict order only on purpose, and say so on screen.

Barbara Tuchman, A Distant Mirror, foreword

Juxtaposition has to mean something

Cutting between strands is not round-robin fairness. Gilligan's rule: if you cut from A to B, it is because the two mean something side by side. Otherwise finish A.

Vince Gilligan, quoted in Neil Landau, The TV Showrunner's Roadmap, ch. 2

Chapter 02 · Shape · Reporting

Depth 3 · Build Ready 8 min deep-dive

02

Research-To-Script.

Research is not a pile. It is a chain of claims that can survive a hostile read.

Full Chapter →

Turn every beat into a checkable claim.

Separate evidence from interpretation.

Write the source stack before the script sings.

Stop researching when the risk is mapped.

Key Rule

No load-bearing sentence enters the script without a source, a visual proof plan, and a risk label. If it cannot meet that bar, it becomes opinion, narration, or trash.

Steal This

Create _Sources.md before the first draft with four headings: Primary, Context, Dissent, Visual Proof. If Dissent is empty, the episode is still shopping for confirmation.

_Sources.md

# Primary  documents · filings · data · interviews

# Context  books · reports · prior coverage

# Dissent  (empty) → still shopping for confirmation

# Visual Proof  zoom · map · chart · archive · quote card

The script is not the first artifact. The first artifact is a claim ledger. Kovach and Rosenstiel put verification at the centre; Harrower turns it into behaviour — check names, titles, numbers, spellings, claims, attribution and disputed facts before the sentence hardens into copy.

Tuchman's Law

Operating Model · Promise, Proof, Pressure

Promise.

What the viewer came for.

Proof.

One concrete image, document, quote, map or number that shows this is not vibes in a blazer.

Pressure.

Why the question cannot stay academic.

One visual idea per beat — when the screen becomes a document, the viewer reads instead of listens (Duarte).

Steal This

Draft the cold open before the full script. If the first 30 seconds cannot state the wound, the evidence, and the turn, the episode is still research, not story.

Skip This If

You already have a proof-first opening that works without music, titles, or explanation. If it collapses when muted or read as plain text, keep cutting.

Five Opens That Fit

01

The contradiction

Two facts that should not coexist. The friction is the story.

02

The object

A document, map, bottle, memo, gate, filing or archival clip carries authority before the narrator litigates.

03

The false promise

Let the audience hear the pitch or myth, then cut to the receipt that makes it rot in public.

04

Scene of consequence

Begin after the damage. Walk backward. Built for war, policy failure, fraud, capture.

05

Personal calibration

Gary's stake only when it sharpens the reporting — why the question matters, not why the host is special.

Three Tests Before Lock

The mute test

Mute the timeline. If the visuals do not clarify the argument, simplify them.

The plain-text test

Read the open aloud and as plain text. If it sounds written, rewrite it; if it collapses without music or titles, keep cutting.

The no-oversell test

Does the full episode deliver exactly what the open implies? Promise a scandal, deliver a policy nuance, and viewers punish the gap.

Failure Modes

The throat-clear. “To understand X, we have to go back…” Usually true. Still a bad first sentence.

The lecture title. “Today we are discussing…” is a meeting agenda wearing foundation.

The fake question. A question the title already answered is filler.

The oversold tease. Promise a scandal, deliver a policy nuance, and viewers punish the gap.

The montage tax. Rapid cuts create pace, not argument. If every shot is a vibe, the viewer learns the editor owns plugins.

“Familiar story. Hidden mechanism. Concrete proof. That is the open.”

04

Voice & Cadence.

The voiceover is not narration poured over the edit. It is the edit's spine, breath, and traffic control.

Full Chapter →

Write for the ear, then cut for the breath.

Slow down around names, numbers, and turns.

Let receipts carry the weight.

Delete jokes that do not expose the system.

Key Rule

Do not score gravity into the sentence. Put gravity in the evidence, then read the sentence like the evidence is enough.

Steal This

Before recording, mark every page with three symbols: slash for breath, box for evidence on screen, underline for the word that cannot blur. An untouched page is not ready for the mic.

The host has three jobs: point to the proof, name the moral math, keep the viewer oriented while the archive, map, document or timeline does the heavy lift. Alburger's voice work keeps returning to intent, breath, posture and emphasis; Weldon's NPR guide adds the small-team habit — read aloud, mark breath breaks, test the room, listen while recording.

Aim at the type, not the man

Mencken on the species: “the yokel par excellence, the booby unmatchable, the king dupe of the cosmos.” An escalating catalog aimed at a class of behaviour. It is funnier than a personal insult and considerably easier to defend.

H. L. Mencken, A Mencken Chrestomathy

Coin the label

Neologisms “let you label issues.” Give each episode's mechanism one coined name (portmanteau, a noun turned verb) and repeat it. The viewer who can name the trick can explain the episode to someone else.

Heinrichs' order of work: reduce the thought to its core first, then decorate. A line that is only decoration dies on the read-aloud.

Nothing required but presence

Key Rule

The cut is not approved because it is smooth. It is approved when the viewer can state what changed, why it mattered, and what proof made it credible.

Steal This

Five-Pass Edit Funnel

01  Paper cutdoes the argument work as text?
02  Spine cutdoes every sequence move the line?
03  Evidence cutdoes proof arrive on the claim?
04  Pace cutenough air at each turn?

Each pass narrows the chaos.Do not make the finish pass do the spine pass's job.

Three Cases · What They Did, Why It Worked, Steal It

01

FRONTLINE — the straight line through a large subject

What they did

Editor Steve Audette (Frame.io interview) reduced pre-9/11 intelligence failure to one character-driven path: John O'Neill in The Man Who Knew. Story boxes on paper; if a scene does not change the line, cut it, even when it is strong.

Why it worked

A single line lets the audience forgive omission. They are promised a coherent way through, not everything.

Steal it

Choose the line before the edit language. Write “This is the story of [mechanism] through [path]” on frame one. Any scene that cannot answer it gets marked red.

02

Last Week Tonight — make the boring thing usable

What they did

The 2014 net-neutrality segment renamed the stakes, ridiculed the incentive structure and sent viewers to a concrete action. Pew's language is careful: strong correlation, not sole cause.

Why it worked

Comedy made attention possible; specificity made action possible. Identify the gatekeeper phrase, translate it into human consequence, give an exact next move.

Steal it

Use comedy when the system hides inside jargon. The joke exposes camouflage, not decorates outrage — then the edit needs a receipt and, when apt, an action.

03

Vox Borders — make the map answer to people

What they did

Per Nieman Lab, season one gathered 7,000 suggestions in a month; for Hong Kong the team expected 40 responses and got 700+, then ran structured callouts before the trip.

Why it worked

Maps make lived politics look clean. Local networks add friction back: how people move, wait, adapt, and read the border from inside.

Steal it

Every map-heavy episode gets a people-layer before the map gets beautiful: who lives inside it, who profits, who is trapped, whose account falsifies the easy explanation.
