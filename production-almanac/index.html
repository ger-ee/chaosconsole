Chapter 01 · ShapeArchitectureDepth 3 · Build Ready9 min deep-dive

Story Architecture

A long episode holds when every turn changes the question the viewer is carrying.

Full chapter →
  • Choose the controlling question first.
  • Build acts around changed understanding.
  • Reset before the viewer gets lost.
  • Cut brilliance that leaves the spine.
Key Rule

Every act must change the viewer's answer to the same question. If an act only adds information, it is probably a folder, not a story turn.

Question is the thing the episode is actually investigating — not the topic. Line is the path through the subject: a person, memo, shipment, border, court case, agency decision or money trail. Turn is the moment understanding changes; a good turn reorganizes the facts already on the table rather than adding one.

Steal This

Before research gets pretty, write the sentence: "This is the story of [mechanism] told through [path], because [cost]." Then make every chapter beat defend its right to stay.

ActViewer questionExit test
Cold openWhy should I trust this click?The wound and proof are visible.
SetupWhat did the public story claim?The official version is clear.
MechanismHow did the system behave?The viewer can explain the engine.
ReversalWhat did we miss?A new fact changes the read.
ConsequenceWho lived with the result?The cost is specific, not atmospheric.
LandingWhat remains unsettled?The viewer knows what was proved and what was not.

Use resets as mercy: after two dense sequences, one plain line that says which door the viewer is about to walk through. Pinker's curse of knowledge belongs in the edit bay — you know the acronyms; the viewer gets them one at a time while eating cereal.

  • The chronology trap. Time order is not story order. Use chronology when cause depends on sequence, not because the folder starts in 1947.
  • The all-star sidebar. A brilliant tangent that does not change the line is still a tangent. Save it, cut it, or make it a separate upload.
  • The fake climax. A loud reveal without changed understanding is a lighting cue pretending to be structure.
  • The moral fog machine. If the viewer cannot state who did what, to whom, with what consequence, the episode is hiding inside mood.

"A chapter earns its length by changing the viewer's map."

Almanac working rule
Story Architecture checklist · 10 items
  1. Write the controlling question before the beat sheet.
  2. Choose the path through the subject: person, mechanism, document, place, or decision chain.
  3. Write the episode spine: question, cost, mechanism, turn, consequence.
  4. Give every act a viewer question and an exit test.
  5. Place reset lines after dense sections.
  6. Cut scenes that add information without changing the line.
  7. Mark chronology-dependent beats separately from optional backstory.
  8. Check that each turn reorganizes what came before.
  9. Cross-link major story beats to source and visual proof plans.
  10. Read only the act headings. If the story vanishes, rebuild the map.
Chapter 02 · ShapeReportingDepth 3 · Build Ready8 min deep-dive

Research-To-Script

Research is not a pile. It is a chain of claims that can survive a hostile read.

Full chapter →
  • Turn every beat into a checkable claim.
  • Separate evidence from interpretation.
  • Write the source stack before the script sings.
  • Stop researching when the risk is mapped.
Key Rule

No load-bearing sentence enters the script without a source, a visual proof plan, and a risk label. If it cannot meet that bar, it becomes opinion, narration, or trash.

The script is not the first artifact. The first artifact is a claim ledger. Kovach and Rosenstiel put verification at the centre; Harrower turns it into behaviour — check names, titles, numbers, spellings, claims, attribution and disputed facts before the sentence hardens into copy.

Steal This

Create _Sources.md before the first draft with four headings: Primary, Context, Dissent, Visual Proof. If Dissent is empty, the episode is still shopping for confirmation.

StageOutputExit test
SeedOne question and one suspected mechanism.You can state what the episode tests.
Claim mapChronological or causal list of claims.Every beat can be true or false.
Source stackPrimary, expert, context, and dissenting sources.No source is doing a job it cannot do.
Beat sheetStory order with proof images assigned.The viewer can follow without a footnote cloud.
Fact passMarked copy: fact, interpretation, opinion, joke.A hostile reader knows what you know and how.
Script lockVO draft ready for visual planning.No unresolved red claims remain.
Ledger columnWhat goes thereWhy it exists
ClaimThe exact sentence or beat.Prevents vague sourcing.
SourceDocument, interview, book, dataset, or clip.Shows where the claim came from.
StatusVerified, disputed, inferred, opinion.Stops uncertainty from wearing a tie.
Visual proofDocument zoom, map, chart, archive, quote card.Hands the editor something real.

Fact pass is a behaviour pass, not copyediting: ask what a motivated critic would attack. "Led to" is stronger than "was followed by"; "proved" is stronger than "suggested". If the evidence only supports "suggested", write that and let the edit carry the pressure. Stop rules: every main claim has a source and a status; every disputed claim has the dispute represented fairly; every visual-proof need has an asset or an honest placeholder. Then stop — production needs a version it can test.

"If the source cannot change the script, it is probably not research anymore."

Almanac working rule
Research-To-Script checklist · 10 items
  1. Write the seed question in one sentence.
  2. Create _Sources.md with Primary, Context, Dissent, and Visual Proof headings.
  3. Convert every major beat into a checkable claim.
  4. Mark each claim as verified, disputed, inferred, opinion, or joke.
  5. Assign one visual proof plan to every load-bearing claim.
  6. Run a hostile read for causality, names, titles, numbers, dates, and attribution.
  7. Represent the strongest dissenting source where the topic is contested.
  8. Move legal, rights, or defamation risks into the ledger before the edit.
  9. Cut claims that cannot be sourced at the needed risk level.
  10. Lock the beat sheet once new research stops changing the story order.
Chapter 03 · HookOpeningsDepth 3 · Build Ready8 min deep-dive

The Cold Open

The first job is not to be clever. The first job is to prove the viewer did not make a mistake.

Full chapter →
  • Open on the wound, not the throat-clear.
  • Show proof by 0:05. Name the question by 0:15. Promise the argument by 0:30.
  • Familiar story, hidden mechanism, concrete proof.
  • Dry speed, not panic speed.
Operating model · Promise, Proof, Pressure

Promise: what the viewer came for. Proof: one concrete image, document, quote, map or number that shows this is not vibes in a blazer. Pressure: why the question cannot stay academic. One visual idea per beat — when the screen becomes a document, the viewer reads instead of listens (Duarte).

Steal This

Draft the cold open before the full script. If the first 30 seconds cannot state the wound, the evidence, and the turn, the episode is still research, not story.

Skip This If

You already have a proof-first opening that works without music, titles, or explanation. If it collapses when muted or read as plain text, keep cutting.

Five opens that fitThe move
The contradictionTwo facts that should not coexist. The friction is the story.
The objectA document, map, bottle, memo, gate, filing or archival clip carries authority before the narrator litigates.
The false promiseLet the audience hear the pitch or myth, then cut to the receipt that makes it rot in public.
Scene of consequenceBegin after the damage. Walk backward. Built for war, policy failure, fraud, capture.
Personal calibrationGary's stake only when it sharpens the reporting — why the question matters, not why the host is special.
  • The throat-clear. "To understand X, we have to go back…" Usually true. Still a bad first sentence.
  • The lecture title. "Today we are discussing…" is a meeting agenda wearing foundation.
  • The fake question. A question the title already answered is filler.
  • The oversold tease. Promise a scandal, deliver a policy nuance, and viewers punish the gap.
  • The montage tax. Rapid cuts create pace, not argument. If every shot is a vibe, the viewer learns the editor owns plugins.

"Familiar story. Hidden mechanism. Concrete proof. That is the open."

Almanac working rule · cadence reference: Camp 14 (controlled compression, not frantic)
Cold Open checklist · 10 items
  1. Write the title and thumbnail promise in one plain sentence.
  2. Choose one proof image that appears before 0:05.
  3. State the contradiction before 0:12.
  4. Ask one controlling question before 0:20.
  5. Promise the investigation before 0:30.
  6. Remove logo stings, greetings, channel housekeeping, and "today we are talking about."
  7. Read the open aloud. If it sounds written, rewrite it.
  8. Mute the timeline. If the visuals do not clarify the argument, simplify them.
  9. Run the no-oversell test: does the full episode deliver exactly what the open implies?
  10. After publish, compare intro retention against the title/thumbnail promise before blaming the algorithm.
Chapter 04 · HookPerformanceDepth 3 · Build Ready9 min deep-dive

Voice & Cadence

The voiceover is not narration poured over the edit. It is the edit's spine, breath, and traffic control.

Full chapter →
  • Write for the ear, then cut for the breath.
  • Slow down around names, numbers, and turns.
  • Let receipts carry the weight.
  • Delete jokes that do not expose the system.
Key Rule

Do not score gravity into the sentence. Put gravity in the evidence, then read the sentence like the evidence is enough.

The host has three jobs: point to the proof, name the moral math, keep the viewer oriented while the archive, map, document or timeline does the heavy lift. Alburger's voice work keeps returning to intent, breath, posture and emphasis; Weldon's NPR guide adds the small-team habit — read aloud, mark breath breaks, test the room, listen while recording.

Steal This

Before recording, mark every page with three symbols: slash for breath, box for evidence on screen, underline for the word that cannot blur. An untouched page is not ready for the mic.

BeatVoice movePicture move
Known factKeep it plain. Do not inflate.Quickest visual confirmation.
New nameGive it air, repeat only if necessary.Show spelling, face, place, or title.
NumberSlow the setup, not the whole paragraph.One graph idea, not six.
TurnStop before the reversal. Let the viewer feel the hinge.Cut to the receipt on the turn.
JokeDry read. No laugh-track rhythm.Let the image do half the insult.
AftermathDrop the performance. Speak like the bill arrived.Hold longer than comfort wants.

Comedy pressure. Kaplan's Straight Line / Wavy Line: the institution is the straight line, the evidence is the wavy line, Gary names the mismatch. "Serious fact, then joke, then serious fact" turns comedy into garnish. If the joke can be removed and the argument gets stronger, it was a hat. Recording: stand when possible; run one technical pass (room, mic position, plosives, sibilance, level, headphones); when a line breaks, restart from the nearest clean thought, not the nearest word.

  • The podcast paragraph. Works in a notebook, dies in the cut. Break it into beats.
  • The courtroom voice. Moral clarity does not require a bass drop.
  • The name blizzard. Three unfamiliar people in one sentence is a receipt jam.
  • The joke after the proof. If the evidence already made the point, the joke reads as insecurity.
  • The wall-to-wall read. Leave room for natural sound, testimony and silence.

"A good Blindspot line sounds inevitable after the receipt appears."

Almanac working rule
Voice & Cadence checklist · 10 items
  1. Read the whole script aloud before recording.
  2. Split any sentence that needs two breaths.
  3. Mark breath slashes, proof boxes, and emphasis underlines.
  4. Circle every proper noun and number.
  5. Slow down only where comprehension needs it.
  6. Place a receipt on screen for every load-bearing claim.
  7. Delete jokes that do not expose a contradiction.
  8. Record standing, or sit forward with a clean breath path.
  9. Monitor with headphones and check plosives before the real take.
  10. Listen to the VO-only export before locking the picture.
Chapter 05 · BuildPictureDepth 3 · Build Ready8 min deep-dive

Visual System

A graphic is useful when it argues. It is expensive wallpaper when it merely glows.

Full chapter →
  • Use visuals as proof, orientation, compression, or consequence.
  • One idea per screen beat.
  • Maps need people, not just borders.
  • Archive choices are editorial claims.
Key Rule

Before adding motion, name the visual job in the timeline: receipt, orientation, compression, or consequence. If you cannot name it, the shot is probably decoration.

Steal This

Put the visual job in the asset filename or marker: receipt_fcc_order, orientation_hong_kong_map, consequence_worker_testimony. It keeps the edit honest when the timeline gets ugly.

Shot logic: Mascelli's continuity grammar survives archival, screen-captured and self-shot footage — a sequence needs a wide idea, a specific detail and a bridge. If Gary is on camera, capture the anchor take, close details, hands, desk objects, document inserts, monitor glow and a clean room-tone plate. Motion graphics work when movement reveals causality: money flows, borders shift, permits appear, names connect.

Visual beatBest useDanger
Document zoomExact language, date, name, or contradiction.Too much text makes VO disappear.
MapOrient power, geography, route, supply, or border pressure.A map without people turns politics into furniture.
TimelineSequence, delay, escalation, or cover-up.Too many nodes creates homework.
Lower thirdSpeaker, role, date, and relevance.Job titles can launder authority.
Archive clipWhat people saw or said at the time.Nostalgia can soften horror.
Data cardOne number readable at speed.Charts are not moral conclusions.

Asset hygiene is part of the look. Keep rights notes with the asset, not in memory — memory lies under deadline. Fewer fonts, fewer transitions, fewer fake-paper overlays, more readable citations. Put source, date and mode on screen when the visual makes an evidentiary claim; a lower-third can be beautiful and still behave like a receipt. Every map answers two questions: what is the viewer supposed to understand geographically, and who experiences that geography as pressure?

"The grade is evidence. Restraint reads as credibility."

Blindspot Binder operating note
Visual System checklist · 10 items
  1. Assign every visual beat one job: receipt, orientation, compression, or consequence.
  2. Pair every load-bearing claim with a visual proof plan.
  3. Use one idea per screen beat.
  4. Keep document cards short enough to read while listening.
  5. Build sequences: wide context, specific proof, bridge, aftermath.
  6. Make maps answer both geography and human pressure.
  7. Keep rights notes, source URLs, and dates beside the asset.
  8. Put source/date/mode on screen for evidentiary visuals.
  9. Delete stock footage that can be swapped without changing meaning.
  10. Run the mute test: if the visual track cannot explain the argument skeleton, rebuild it.
Chapter 06 · BuildAssemblyDepth 3 · Build Ready8 min deep-dive

Edit Grammar

Cut on argument first. Rhythm is what happens after the spine exists.

Full chapter →
  • Build a spine cut before a pretty cut.
  • Cut anything off the controlling line.
  • Use pace to protect comprehension.
  • Lock only after picture and proof agree.
Key Rule

The cut is not approved because it is smooth. It is approved when the viewer can state what changed, why it mattered, and what proof made it credible.

Steal This

Colour-code markers by job: claim, proof, turn, breath, risk. Five proof markers in a row and no breath means the viewer is drowning politely.

Pace: fast edits when the viewer already understands the category; slow edits when a new name, number, causal link or moral consequence must land. Use a consequence hold after the big reveal — dryness works when the edit is brave enough to stop moving. Lock rules: never polish a sequence with an unresolved claim; colour, sound and lower thirds make a weak argument harder to kill because it starts looking expensive. Before lock, export a VO-only pass, a mute-picture pass and a full pass.

CutUse it forDo not use it for
Receipt cutDocument, quote, chart, screen capture.Hiding an unsupported claim.
Orientation cutMap, timeline, cast card, location.Filling dead air.
Consequence holdAftermath, silence, testimony, human cost.Sentimental overreach.
Comic cutExpose a contradiction faster than explanation.Changing the subject.
Bridge cutMove over time, place, or missing coverage.Smearing chronology.
Compression montagePattern, scale, repetition.Pretending pace equals evidence.

"Smooth is not structure. Pace is not proof."

Almanac working rule · Audette / FRONTLINE: if a scene does not affect the straight line, it can go
Edit Grammar checklist · 10 items
  1. Build the paper cut before the timeline gets beautiful.
  2. Write the controlling line on the sequence marker.
  3. Delete scenes that do not change the controlling line.
  4. Assign every cut a job: receipt, orientation, consequence, comic, bridge, or compression.
  5. Place proof at the sentence that needs proof.
  6. Slow down for new names, numbers, causal turns, and aftermath.
  7. Colour-code markers for claim, proof, turn, breath, and risk.
  8. Run a VO-only pass for breath and syntax.
  9. Run a mute-picture pass for visual argument.
  10. Lock the sequence before colour, sound, titles, and export polish.
Chapter 07 · BuildDeliveryDepth 3 · Build Ready8 min deep-dive

Sound & Finish

If the viewer strains to hear the argument, the argument has already lost authority.

Full chapter →
  • Dialogue clarity beats cinematic mud.
  • Room tone is production insurance.
  • Music should frame proof, not bully it.
  • Export to YouTube's stated specs.
Key Rule

Do the clarity pass before the taste pass. If dialogue, citations, captions, and source clips are not intelligible, music choice is a distraction with better branding.

Steal This

Record a 20-second room-tone strip for every VO session and label it with date, mic, room and project. Put it in 01_sources/audio, not in the editor's memory.

Capture: quietest room available; kill HVAC, fridge, desk rattles, chair squeaks, browser pings, fabric noise; then record room tone before moving anything. Mix: silence after a document reveal can do more than a swell. Keep foley literal — typewriter, paper, projector — precision, not spy-trailer cosplay. Captions are editorial: auto-captions are a draft; build a term sheet for recurring names and use it across SRTs, lower thirds, title cards and descriptions.

Export checkYouTube-safe defaultSource
ContainerMP4, fast-start behaviour where available.YouTube Help
VideoH.264, progressive, High Profile, 4:2:0. 8 Mbps at 1080p standard frame rate; 35–45 Mbps at 4K.YouTube Help
Frame rateUpload at the same frame rate recorded.YouTube Help
AudioAAC-LC, 48 kHz sample rate, stereo listed at 384 kbps.YouTube Help
ColourBT.709 for SDR uploads.YouTube Help
ArchiveHigh-quality master + project files + SRT in the episode folder.Anderson / Binder

Do not treat YouTube's encoding page as a loudness spec. Use it for container, codec, colour, frame rate and bitrate. Loudness targets belong in the local QC workflow and should be tested against real playback.

"Sound finish is not polish. It is proof that the episode can survive playback."

Almanac working rule
Sound & Finish checklist · 10 items
  1. Kill room noise before recording.
  2. Record and label room tone for every VO session.
  3. Restart failed takes from a complete thought.
  4. Check VO intelligibility before music taste.
  5. Keep source audio audible when it carries proof.
  6. Use silence after major receipts or consequences.
  7. Hand-check captions for names, dates, agencies, and acronyms.
  8. Export a high-quality master and a platform upload file.
  9. Match YouTube's current upload recommendations for codec, frame rate, audio sample rate, and colour.
  10. Archive master, SRT, source notes, project files, and upload file in the episode folder.
Chapter 08 · ShipOperationsDepth 3 · Build Ready8 min deep-dive

Production Logistics

The solo workflow has to be boring on purpose. Boring is where the episode survives.

Full chapter →
  • Make folders before inspiration arrives.
  • Separate source, work, export, and social assets.
  • Shoot for the edit, not the ego.
  • Ship a clean version before polishing the ghost.
Key Rule

The episode is not "in production" until its folder, source ledger, working edit, thumbnail draft, and export target exist. Otherwise the project is still a pile with a dramatic name.

Steal This

Name files blindspot_YYYYMMDD_episode_slug_v01.ext. Version numbers are not glamorous. Neither is rebuilding a lost lower third at midnight.

The 70% rule is a local operating heuristic, not an empirical law: once the episode is source-safe, coherent, audible and visually legible, ship the best clean version instead of waiting for the perfect one to walk in holding a lav mic. Solo capture: sound first — bad picture can become style, bad audio becomes distrust. Light for consistency before drama; a repeatable desk setup beats a heroic look that cannot be rebuilt next Tuesday. Handoff: every review export answers three questions — what changed, what feedback is needed, what is locked. Never ask for "thoughts" when you need a fact check.

FolderWhat goes thereExit test
01_sourcesPDFs, clips, interview audio, screen captures, rights notes.Every load-bearing asset has provenance.
02_workProject files, scripts, graphics, audio sessions, edit files.Current version is obvious.
03_exportsMasters, review files, captions, upload files.Public, review, and archive files are separated.
04_socialThumbnails, shorts, clips, titles, pinned-comment drafts.Packaging is not an afterthought.
_archiveOld versions, killed sequences, superseded research.Nothing important is deleted under stress.

"A clean folder is not admin. It is future evidence."

Almanac working rule
Production Logistics checklist · 10 items
  1. Create the episode folder before drafting.
  2. Add 01_sources, 02_work, 03_exports, 04_social, and _archive.
  3. Create _Sources.md with provenance, rights, risk, and visual proof notes.
  4. Use date, slug, and version number in every production file.
  5. Draft title and thumbnail promise during shaping, not after export.
  6. Capture anchor, detail, insert, empty plate, and room tone for self-shot scenes.
  7. Monitor audio with headphones before real takes.
  8. Label exports by use: review, master, upload, social, caption.
  9. Ask reviewers for one specific kind of feedback.
  10. Archive the master, upload, SRT, thumbnail, source ledger, and postmortem after publish.
Chapter 09 · ShipPublishingDepth 3 · Build Ready9 min deep-dive

Distribution & Algorithm

Packaging is not the opposite of journalism. It is the promise journalism has to keep in public.

Full chapter →
  • Title and thumbnail make a promise. The intro proves or breaks it.
  • CTR without AVD is a trap.
  • Diagnose before changing packaging.
  • One postmortem lesson per upload.
Key Stat

YouTube Help says half of all channels and videos have impressions CTR between 2% and 10%. New videos, small view counts, traffic sources and broad Home impressions vary widely. Compare against the channel and traffic source before declaring victory or doom. High CTR with low average view duration is the platform's own definition of clickbait.

Steal This

Write the packaging promise in one sentence before scripting: "A viewer who clicks this expects to learn why [familiar story] was really about [hidden mechanism]." Then make the cold open pay that bill.

SignalRead it as
Low CTRPromise problem — title/thumbnail. Thumbnail does one job: contradiction, object, or consequence. Not Gary stunned beside a flag.
Low introOpening problem, packaging mismatch, or both.
Mid dipStructure — a name cluster, history dump, or a joke that changed the subject. Dips are edit notes with witnesses.
Low AVDPace. Spikes are not always praise: rewatch or confusion — check whether they replayed a dense chart like a hostage proof-of-life tape.
A/B testsUp to three title/thumbnail variants; highest watch time wins; changing either mid-test stops the test. Compare promises, not moods.

Postmortem: log the packaging promise, thumbnail concept, title, source of early traffic, intro retention read, major dips, major spikes and one edit lesson. Keep it short enough that it happens. Do not build a channel theory from one upload; the next episode inherits one operational change, not ten.

"The algorithm did not betray the promise. The promise either held or it did not."

Almanac working rule · YouTube added watch time to recommendations in 2012 because clicks alone did not prove satisfaction
Distribution & Algorithm checklist · 10 items
  1. Write the title/thumbnail promise before scripting.
  2. Make the cold open pay that promise by 30 seconds.
  3. Choose a thumbnail job: contradiction, object, or consequence.
  4. Compare CTR with traffic source and channel context.
  5. Read low intro retention as a promise/opening diagnosis.
  6. Review dips for comprehension breaks, not just "boring parts."
  7. Review spikes for both interest and confusion.
  8. Run title/thumbnail tests only when the data can teach something.
  9. Do not change multiple packaging variables unless the test is designed for it.
  10. Write one postmortem lesson and carry it into the next episode.

Applied Theft Maps

Not tributes. What they did, why it worked, and what The Blindspot can steal without wearing the costume.

Full case files →

OliverJargon translated into action.
VoxMaps corrected by people inside them.
BlindspotSteal the method. Leave the costume.
Case 01

FRONTLINE — the straight line through a large subject

What they did

Editor Steve Audette (Frame.io interview) reduced pre-9/11 intelligence failure to one character-driven path: John O'Neill in The Man Who Knew. Story boxes on paper; if a scene does not change the line, cut it, even when it is strong.

Why it worked

A single line lets the audience forgive omission. They are promised a coherent way through, not everything.

Steal it

Choose the line before the edit language. Write "This is the story of [mechanism] through [path]" on frame one. Any scene that cannot answer it gets marked red.

Case 02

Last Week Tonight — make the boring thing usable

What they did

The 2014 net-neutrality segment renamed the stakes, ridiculed the incentive structure and sent viewers to a concrete action. Pew's language is careful: strong correlation, not sole cause.

Why it worked

Comedy made attention possible; specificity made action possible. Identify the gatekeeper phrase, translate it into human consequence, give an exact next move.

Steal it

Use comedy when the system hides inside jargon. The joke exposes camouflage, not decorates outrage — then the edit needs a receipt and, when apt, an action.

Case 03

Vox Borders — make the map answer to people

What they did

Per Nieman Lab, season one gathered 7,000 suggestions in a month; for Hong Kong the team expected 40 responses and got 700+, then ran structured callouts before the trip.

Why it worked

Maps make lived politics look clean. Local networks add friction back: how people move, wait, adapt, and read the border from inside.

Steal it

Every map-heavy episode gets a people-layer before the map gets beautiful: who lives inside it, who profits, who is trapped, whose account falsifies the easy explanation.

What not to steal

Not FRONTLINE's institutional distance when the episode needs a host voice. Not Oliver's performance size when the evidence is not comic. Not Vox's visual language when the map becomes cleaner than the politics. Steal the production logic; leave the costumes on the rack.

Case File theft checklist · 10 items
  1. Identify the case's reusable production move.
  2. Separate structure from style.
  3. State the Blindspot adaptation in one sentence.
  4. For FRONTLINE-style subjects, choose a single path through the system.
  5. For Oliver-style subjects, translate jargon into consequence and action.
  6. For Vox-style maps, build the local-source layer before final graphics.
  7. Mark correlation claims as correlation unless the source proves causation.
  8. Write what not to steal before the episode copies the wrong surface.
  9. Add one case-file lesson to the episode checklist.
  10. Delete homage that does not improve proof, clarity, or retention.
